# D7. How other fields get work done through others with little attention

Follow `docs/design/from-zero/briefs/common-data.md`. It overrides anything below. You need web search for this task. If you have no working web search or fetch tool, send `worker_done` at once saying so, and do nothing else.

Another worker (D5) is checking the evidence behind the method's principles. Your job is different: collect the actual working procedures of fields that solved this problem long ago, in enough detail to copy. Do not coordinate with them.

## Why

One person directing many hands, with scarce attention and costly mistakes, is an old problem. Some fields have procedures refined over decades. The method in `docs/design/from-zero-20261112.md` borrowed ten principles from them by reasoning. We now need the procedures themselves: the forms, the wording, the timings, the rules of thumb, and the known ways each goes wrong.

## Fields to study (at least ten; add better ones if you find them)

1. A chief of staff or private office serving one principal: briefing books, decision memos, the bring-forward file, "unless otherwise directed", what staff may decide alone.
2. Military orders: commander's intent, mission-type orders, the back-brief, the five-paragraph order, rehearsal.
3. Surgery and anaesthesia: the checklist, the time-out, closed-loop communication.
4. Hospital wards: structured handover, ward boards, escalation rules, alarm fatigue and what fixed it.
5. Aviation: the dark cockpit, crew callouts, sterile cockpit, graded autonomy for new pilots, air traffic flight strips.
6. Construction and architecture: sketch, drawing, mock-up, the punch list, the walk-through, sign-off.
7. Restaurant kitchens: the pass, the expediter, calling orders back, tickets, "hands".
8. Newsrooms and publishing: the desk, the budget meeting, copy flow, the editor's mark-up.
9. Film and theatre production: the call sheet, dailies, the stage manager's prompt book.
10. Law and consulting: delegation to juniors, the partner's review, precedent files, standing instructions from clients.
11. Manufacturing: stop-the-line cords, visual management, work-in-progress limits, tightened and reduced inspection.
12. Emergency dispatch and incident command: one commander, span of control, status boards, read-back.

## For each field

- The procedure in concrete terms: who says what to whom, on what form or in what words, when. Quote real templates and phraseology where you can find them.
- What it costs the person in charge: how many times they are involved, for how long.
- What it protects against, and the evidence that it works.
- How it is known to fail, and what was done about it.
- What carries over to one person directing software helpers, and what does not.

## Then

- The **ten procedures most worth copying**, each written as a rule a designer could apply tomorrow, with its source.
- **Wordings worth copying**: the exact forms of words that make an instruction, a read-back, a question or a report short and safe.
- **Numbers worth knowing**: spans of control, limits on items in a briefing, alarm rates that people can live with, sampling rates as trust grows.
- **Warnings**: what these fields learned the hard way that a new design is likely to repeat.
