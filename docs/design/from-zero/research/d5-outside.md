# D5 — Outside evidence and prior art

**10 principles, 3 gaps: 1 doubtful absolute, 12 hold with limits.** The strongest numbers are a 23% medical-error reduction after a handoff bundle, no consistent late-fix penalty across 171 software projects, and a default effect of d=0.68 across 58 comparisons. These are published estimates, not estimates of this method's benefit. The complete method remains untested.
Research snapshots: 2026-11-11–12. Source keys below distinguish experiments, field observations, research syntheses, professional practice and user reports. “Change” means a design inference, not a tested intervention.

## 1. Principles and gaps

| Principle or gap | Verdict | Best support; strongest limit or contrary evidence | What to change |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 Attention is the only scarce thing | doubtful | Simon makes the attention-allocation argument, not an exclusive-resource theorem (S01). Automation can leave harder recovery work to people (S09); agent teams also consume coordination effort and tokens (S32). | Minimise attention subject to correctness, harm, cost and recovery limits; count setup and recovery time too. |
| P2 React rather than specify | holds with a limit | Seven-team prototype experiment: about 45% less effort; lower functionality/robustness and better usability; specification teams integrated more easily (S02, primary abstract). Preferences can be constructed by elicitation (S06). | Show cheap alternatives; keep written constraints and acceptance examples. Remove “the person never writes a specification”; sketches cannot settle hidden requirements. |
| P3 Close misunderstanding before work | holds with a limit | I-PASS: 10,740 admissions, errors 24.5→18.8 per 100, 23% relative reduction; bundled before/after study, not read-back alone (S04). Army distinguishes immediate confirmation from a later plan backbrief (S03); 171-project study found no consistent late-fix effort penalty (S05). | Confirm intent and authority before expensive commitments, then reopen on new facts. No universal defect multiplier, one-minute guarantee, or once-only agreement. |
| P4 Decide once | holds with a limit | Private-office practice preserves decisions with their submissions (S22); preference research finds context-sensitive elicitation (S06). Neither proves one answer is a permanent preference. | Store ruling, scope, reason and exceptions; distinguish explicit standing authority from inferred preference; allow correction and expiry. |
| P5 Believable silence | holds with a limit | In one ICU study, 88.8% of 12,671 annotated arrhythmia alarms were false positives (S08). Automation experiments expose out-of-loop recovery problems (S09); reliance must track capability/context (S07). | Quiet by default, but show observation age, next checkpoint and “unknown/unobserved”; independently detect missed commitments and monitor failure. No heartbeat transcript for the person. |
| P6 No silent or indefinite waits | holds with a limit | Default meta-analysis: 58 comparisons, pooled n=73,675, d=0.68 [95% CI 0.53–0.83], with large variation and some reversed effects (S10). This measures selection, not informed agreement or safe timeout actions. | Every wait gets owner, consequence and return time. A timeout may act only inside existing authority; otherwise park/escalate. Make overturning cheap; silence is not fresh consent. |
| P7 Show the thing; independent checking | holds with a limit | Fagan reports benefits from structured inspections and follow-up, not merely adding another person (S11). Medication double-check review: 13 studies; insufficient evidence for lower errors/harm; independence often unspecified (S12). | Show result plus criterion-level evidence and unresolved assumptions; use risk-based checks with distinct evidence, reproducible tests and independent judgement. Do not assert a second agent guarantees quality. |
| P8 One counterpart, one doorway | holds with a limit | Private offices prepare advice and preserve decisions (S22–23); shared team leads exist (S32). The same tool documents fixed leadership, status lag and resumption limits; a single lead does not remove coordination (S32). | One entry point, durable underlying record, named backup and direct escalation. Let the person inspect original evidence and challenge the counterpart's filter. |
| P9 Earned, task-specific trust | holds with a limit | NIST skip-lot practice requires demonstrated quality, consecutive acceptable lots, random sampling and return to normal inspection after rejection (S13). Clinical entrustment depends on task, context, supervisor and trainee (S14). | Autonomy follows repeated relevant outcomes and consequence severity; keep random audits and reset on material changes. One acceptance is insufficient; acceptance is not proof of absence of defects. |
| P10 Limit work in progress | holds with a limit | Little proves L=λW under stated finite/stationary-process conditions (S15); switching experiments find attention residue (S16). Memory synthesis concerns about four chunks under controlled conditions, not seven projects (S19). | Cap expected human review minutes and queue age, not an arbitrary item count. Match arrivals to observed service capacity; leave room for variability, urgent work and dependencies. |
| Intent gap | holds with a limit | Closed-loop check-backs explicitly require sender confirmation of the receiver's understanding (S37); I-PASS supports structured handoff bundles (S04). Prototype tradeoffs and constructed preferences mean intent evolves (S02, S06). | Include constraints, authority, acceptance examples and contingent plans; recognise discovery rather than call every revision waste. |
| Sight gap | holds with a limit | At two EDs, whiteboard inaccuracies were 1%/3%, versus 13%/12% on electronic boards; observational, not proof that paper is superior (S25). Agent task status can lag (S32). | Separate reported, observed and checked state; timestamp evidence; stale/failed observation is a visible state. A dashboard is not itself observability. |
| Trust gap | holds with a limit | Appropriate-reliance research supports calibration, not maximal trust (S07); clinical entrustment also depends on competence and situation (S14). “What I would do” is not a correctness test. | Track capability, authority and incentives separately from honesty. The three gaps are useful labels, not an established exhaustive theory of delegation costs. |

## 2. Missing principles

- **Make recovery and refusal first-class.** Human-AI guidance includes efficient dismissal/correction and global controls (S21); out-of-loop research warns against expecting instant expert rescue (S09). Add stop, amend, revoke authority, retain evidence and restore a known state; rehearse failures before reducing supervision.
- **Protect dissent and bad-news reporting.** In 51 manufacturing teams, psychological safety was associated with learning behaviour, not established as a causal effect (S20). Do not penalise useful questions or reopened agreements as excess cycles; give checkers a route around the counterpart.
- **Coordinate dependencies, not just owner attention.** Restaurant fieldwork finds concurrency/compatibility problems across routines (S29); agent-team guidance warns about shared files and dependencies (S32). Reserve resources and check interfaces before parallel work starts; reconcile the integrated result.
- **External memory needs a credible return plan.** Experiments found specific plans reduced unfinished-goal interference (S18); a stored item alone was not the intervention. Show who will resume it, when/on what event, and whether the reminder channel is working.
- **Measure outcomes and the operator's state.** Interrupted participants sometimes worked faster while reporting more stress/effort (S17). Track escaped errors, rework, waiting age, intervention/recovery minutes and subjective load alongside answers/cycles; fewer prompts alone can mean less oversight.

## 3. Patterns to borrow

Attention costs below are qualitative task demands, not measured time savings; failure modes with no direct observation in the cited source are labelled inference.

| Pattern; origin | What the person sees; attention cost | Works where; known limit or design trap |
|---|---|---|
| Prepared decision packet; executive/private-office briefings (S22–23) | Decision sought, recommendation, options, risks, supporting record; read the fork and choose. | Helps busy accountable decision-makers; preparation moves work to staff. Keep original advice available: filtering loss is an inference, not a quantified benefit/cost. |
| Persistent strips; air traffic control (S24) | Compact flight/work objects that remain available for interaction; scan, annotate and coordinate. | Supports dynamic shared work; use varies with staffing and coordination style. Preserve interaction and shared awareness when digitising; merely replacing paper is not the intervention. |
| Shared operational board; hospital ED/ward coordination (S25) | Owner, location, pending action and time; occasional glance plus continual staff maintenance. | Useful for handoffs and pending actions; observed electronic boards were less used and less accurate. Freshness and responsibility matter more than medium. |
| Editorial desk and flowline; newsroom field study (S26) | Story cards, stages and planned publication; scan exceptions and make editorial tradeoffs. | Shared stage meanings supported autonomy; management's earlier colour scheme confused staff. Agree categories with workers; “written” and “published” must remain distinct. |
| Located defect and reinspection; construction punch lists (S27) | A defect on the actual work, responsible correction, then reinspection; walk/inspect specific items. | Contract closeout; owner inspection follows contractor preparation. Hidden or unlisted defects remain a limitation (inference); avoid making final inspection the first check. |
| Pass and expeditor; restaurant practice/fieldwork (S28–29) | Orders, assembled plates, shortages and readiness; one coordinator matches tickets to results and calls exceptions. | Coordinates timing and complete deliveries; live synchronisation needs effort. The expeditor can also do other work: this does not establish a dedicated non-doing counterpart. |
| Isolated runs with a review surface; Codex/Cursor official write-ups (S30–31) | Parallel run conversations and changed software/evidence; choose a run, inspect its output, integrate it. | Separates execution from attention; human review/integration can become the queue (inference). Cursor users reported live runs absent from app lists although direct links worked (S36). |
| Lead plus shared tasks and drill-down; Claude Code teams (S32) | Lead conversation, agent panel/shared work and individual transcripts; read synthesis, open exceptions. | Independent investigations; docs warn about coordination overhead, status lag and resumption. Reports describe lost post-compaction completion delivery (S34) and noisy idle/peer notifications (S35). |
| Actionable wait versus ordinary event; AI-helper complaints (S33–35) | Approval/input request distinct from completion/idle information; every interruption demands a decision or context reload. | One Codex reporter found approvals consumed attention even with two sessions (S33). Borrow explicit authority and exception routing; do not infer that security approvals should disappear. |

## 4. Traps

- **A quiet broken observer looks like success.** Distinguish “checked healthy”, “not yet due”, “blocked” and “unknown”; observed app-list omissions and lost completion reports show why a single delivery surface needs reconciliation (S34, S36).
- **Rubber-stamped checks share the maker's blind spot.** Give the checker requirements and evidence before the maker's preferred conclusion; collect whether checks actually catch defects (S11–12; proposed design response).
- **Default uptake masquerades as preference.** Defaults affect selection; repeated recommended answers are not unbiased evidence that questions were unnecessary (S06, S10).
- **A polished summary removes the decisive uncertainty.** Preserve source evidence, omissions and disagreements; this is an inference from calibration/correction guidance, not a measured summary-loss rate (S07, S21).
- **Idle chatter and repetitive permissions recreate a full-time dispatcher.** User reports establish these failure modes, not their prevalence or present product-wide status (S33–35).
- **A single doorway becomes a single bottleneck or failure point.** Keep backup, inspectable evidence and a bypass for safety/disagreement; an inference from lead/resumption limits and learning research (S20, S32).
- **Optimising cycle count hides quality loss.** Discovery, necessary escalation and useful disagreement are not automatically waste; speed, stress and quality can diverge (S02, S17, S20).
